PM Portfolio · Case Study

Spare: saving as a side effect
of spending

A behavioral fintech product that makes saving automatic — and why getting the withdrawal experience right matters more than anything else in the product.

Behavioral Fintech · MVP India · Android-first Urban 22–35 · ₹40–70k/month Pre-launch · April 2026 Role: Product Manager
TL;DR
  • Spare automatically saves ₹5–₹50 every time a user makes a UPI transaction — zero effort after setup, zero guilt for spending.
  • Reddit analysis of ~2,000 posts surfaced the real problem: users weren't failing at saving because they lacked tools. They were failing because every savings tool made them feel worse. That reframe drove the entire product strategy.
  • The most important product surface isn't onboarding — it's withdrawal. Every fintech product that adds friction to exiting is signaling it doesn't trust users with their own money. Spare does the opposite.
  • MVP is deliberately narrow: one loop, one user type, no new features until D60 retention is validated. Every cut was a decision, not a deferral.
01 Problem

The failure isn't intent. It's the system.

India's urban middle class knows they should save. The problem isn't awareness — it's execution. Savings apps, goal-setting tools, budgeting trackers: the industry has kept building more features. Retention rates tell the story — most Indian personal finance apps see D30 retention below 25%. The tools exist. People still aren't saving.

The real failure is structural: every saving mechanism creates a decision moment — a point where the user must actively choose saving over the present. That moment consistently loses. Not because people are irresponsible, but because present-moment attention reliably beats future-state intentions, especially under financial stress.

There's also an emotional layer that most products make worse, not better. Research into user conversations around saving (covered in §02) surfaced a consistent pattern: the apps that users abandoned weren't abandoned because they were hard to use. They were abandoned because opening them triggered guilt. Every "over budget" alert, every missed saving target, made the user feel like they'd failed again. The product claimed to help. It made anxiety worse.

The target user hasn't failed at saving. They've tried every product that claimed it would help — and been let down by each one.

02 Research & Insights

What user data actually said

Before committing to the core mechanics, I ran a lightweight discovery process: scraped and analyzed ~2,000 posts and comments from Reddit communities focused on personal finance (r/personalfinance, r/IndiaInvestments) using keyword queries around "saving money," "can't save," "budgeting apps," and "I keep failing." The goal wasn't statistical significance — it was pattern recognition. Were the assumptions in the brief confirmed by real user language, or were we building on intuition?

Method
Keyword scraping → thematic clustering → emotional signal extraction. ~2,000 posts analyzed. Outputs informed the problem framing, the UX principles, and three specific product decisions.
Pattern 01
Failure was attributed to discipline, not systems
"I just don't have the willpower." "I know I should save but I can't stick to it." "Every month I tell myself this is the month."
→ Users had internalized saving failure as a personal flaw. The product needed to externalize the mechanism — make saving something that happened to them, not something they did.
Pattern 02
Apps were abandoned after guilt — not after a bad feature
"I deleted Walnut because every time I opened it I felt bad." "The budgeting thing made me feel worse, not better." "I stopped checking because it stressed me out."
→ The emotional experience of existing apps was the churn trigger — not usability. This confirmed that removing shame architecture wasn't a nice-to-have; it was the product strategy.
Pattern 03
Unexpected expenses were cited as the specific failure point
"I was doing great until my bike needed repair." "One bill and the whole plan fell apart." "I can't commit to a fixed amount because income isn't predictable."
→ Fixed saving commitments (SIPs, auto-debits) broke on irregular expense months. A transaction-triggered model naturally adapts — you save more in high-spend weeks and less in lean ones.
Pattern 04
Trust in digital financial products was fragile and specific
"How do I know I can get it back?" "What if the app shuts down?" "I don't want my money sitting somewhere I can't see."
→ The fear wasn't about saving — it was about access. This directly shaped the withdrawal design: visible balance at all times, always-accessible withdraw button, zero-friction exit. Not UX polish — trust architecture.
How research changed the product: We entered discovery thinking the core problem was habit formation. The data reframed it: the problem was emotional — guilt, avoidance, and anxiety were doing more damage than the lack of a good saving tool. This shifted the product from "better saving mechanism" to "anxiety-free saving mechanism." Every downstream decision follows from that reframe.
03 Core Insight

Spending is not the enemy of saving. It is the trigger.

Instead of asking users to choose between spending and saving, make spending the mechanism of saving. Every UPI transaction automatically generates a small save — 5–10% of the transaction value, capped at ₹50. No user action required after setup.

"Make saving easier" still requires a choice. "Automatic" removes the choice category altogether. The product never competes with present-moment attention because it never asks for any attention at all.

The research confirmed why this reframe matters emotionally: users weren't failing at saving because they lacked tools — they were failing because every saving tool reminded them they'd failed. By making spending the trigger, the product converts a guilt moment into a win. A user who spent ₹500 on dinner saved ₹40 in the process. The emotional register shifts from deprivation to accumulation — and that shift is what makes the habit stick.

The moat, if it works, is behavioral: Once a user reliably associates spending with saving over 60+ days, that association is hard to transfer to a competitor. The growing savings balance creates additional switching cost. Features can be copied. Internalized habits are harder to dislodge.
04 Product Strategy

Differentiation through subtraction

Vision: A saving product that works for people who have already failed at every other saving product — not by making them more disciplined, but by removing the need for discipline entirely.

Target user: Salaried urban professional, 22–35, ₹40–70k/month. Has tried budgeting apps. Dropped them within weeks. Believes they should save but doesn't act on it. The MVP is explicitly scoped to regular-income users — gig workers and freelancers have different mechanics and are a v1.1 problem, not because they're less important but because building for both simultaneously would compromise the core experience for both.

Core principles (derived from research): Zero setup before first value. No shame states anywhere in the product. No streak mechanics — they punish the irregular spending behavior this user actually has. Withdrawal is a feature, not a failure mode. These came directly from what we saw in the data: products that triggered shame lost users; products that rewarded any behavior kept them.

vs. Acorns: The closest comparable is Acorns in the US. The meaningful difference isn't mechanism — it's emotional frame. Acorns is a savings product that tolerates spending. Spare is built around spending as the core mechanic. That inversion changes every downstream decision: the home screen, the notification copy, what counts as success, and how we think about the exit.

05 Behavior Loop

The system that runs without the user

01 · Trigger
Spend
UPI transaction detected via SMS parsing. Under 10s end-to-end latency required — beyond that, the behavioral link between spending and saving breaks.
02 · Action
Auto-save
5–10% of transaction value, ₹5–₹50 cap, moved to savings pool. Excludes rent, EMI, utilities. Silent skip if balance <₹500.
03 · Reward
Notification
Warm push within seconds. 30+ variants to prevent habituation. Always frames accumulation — never the triggering transaction.
04 · Repeat
Next spend
Loop runs passively. No user action required. No "saving paused" state shown — that would introduce the anxiety the product is designed to remove.

The loop is intentionally fragile in one place: latency. If the transaction-to-notification delay exceeds ~10 seconds, users have moved on mentally and the reinforcement doesn't land. This is the single most important technical constraint in the product.

06 Real-World Constraints

What made this harder than it looks

The product concept is simple. The execution has real constraints that shaped almost every design decision.

Technical
SMS parsing is unreliable
Indian banks use inconsistent SMS formats. Parsing accuracy across all major banks in MVP was estimated at ~85–90% — meaning roughly 1 in 8 transactions would be missed. We spec'd a bank API fallback for day 1, but regulatory approval added 6+ weeks to the timeline. The MVP launched on SMS parsing alone, with known gaps.
Technical
Notification latency floor
The behavioral reinforcement requires <10s from transaction to notification. In practice, SMS parsing + transfer initiation + push delivery averaged 6–8s in testing — within target, but with no buffer. Any backend latency spike breaks the loop. This became a non-negotiable SLA for the infrastructure team.
Regulatory
Auto fund movement requires RBI approval
Automatic transfer of user funds requires operating as a licensed NBFC or partnering with one. The product launched with a partner model — slower transfers (T+1 to T+2), higher transaction costs, and less control over the withdrawal experience. The partner constraint directly affected how we designed the withdrawal flow.
Trust
Indian fintech trust baseline is low
The target demographic is acutely sensitive to products that feel like they trap money — a lived experience from informal chit funds and early digital wallets. Any friction in the withdrawal flow wasn't a UX problem; it was a fundamental trust problem. This is why we treated the withdrawal spec as a primary surface, not an edge case.
Product
Transaction categorization without ML
Excluding rent, EMI, and utilities required merchant categorization. In MVP, without ML infrastructure, we relied on keyword matching in SMS bodies — effective for ~80% of cases but failing on non-standard billers. Some users saw saves on utility payments in the first week. We added a manual exclusion option as a fast fix.
Product
Fraud risk vs. zero-friction withdrawal
Zero-friction withdrawal (no re-auth under ₹10,000) was a deliberate trust decision — but it created a fraud surface. A compromised account could drain savings without the user noticing in time. We added anomaly detection on the backend (withdrawal frequency + device fingerprinting), but this was a real tradeoff, not a solved problem.
07 MVP Definition

What was built — and what was deliberately cut

Built in MVP
  • UPI transaction detection via SMS parsing
  • Automatic micro-save engine (5–10%, ₹5–₹50)
  • Variable push notification library (30+ variants)
  • Home screen: balance + recent saves + withdraw CTA
  • Full withdrawal flow (3 taps, no friction)
  • Low-balance silent skip (₹500 threshold)
  • Pause saving toggle
  • 2-screen onboarding (<60 seconds)
Intentionally cut
  • Budgeting or spend tracking
  • Spending categories or analytics
  • Multiple savings goals
  • Gamification — badges, streaks, leaderboards
  • Investment products
  • Social or shared goals
  • Bank API integration (v1.1)
  • iOS (v1.1)
The exclusion test: Any proposed feature had to clear one bar — does it add a user decision, introduce a negative feedback state, or complicate the home screen? If yes, it waited. This rule was applied literally to about a dozen reasonable feature requests, including in-app charts, saving goals, and a "streak" mechanic suggested by an advisor.
08 Key Product Decisions

Where judgment was required

D–01
Automation vs. control — who sets the save amount?
Problem
The save amount needed to feel real enough to matter but small enough not to alarm. Research showed that users who tried apps with manual saving controls either set them to the minimum or never revisited the setting — both producing near-zero saving behavior.
Options
Full user control (Acorns model). System-decided flat amount. Percentage-based algorithm with a user-adjustable low/medium/high band.
Tradeoff
More user control adds activation friction and typically produces worse outcomes. But a flat ₹50 cap applied identically to a ₹500 dinner and a ₹12,000 grocery run feels inconsistent — and inconsistency erodes trust in the algorithm over time.
Decision: System-decided (5–10%, ₹5–₹50 cap) with two refinements grounded in user research: (1) new users start conservative for the first two weeks — we needed trust before we could take more, because research showed users attributed saving failures to the product feeling "grabby"; and (2) rent, EMI, and utility transactions excluded entirely — saving on fixed obligations felt punitive in every user test and echoed the "unexpected expense disruption" pattern we saw in the data.
D–02
First value / activation — when does the product prove itself?
Problem
A passive-trigger product has a cold-start problem. A new user may not transact for 12–24 hours after install. Their first experience of Spare is silence — and silence reads as "this doesn't work." Research showed users gave passive finance products very short trust windows before concluding they were broken.
Options
Wait for the first organic transaction. Seed a ₹10–₹20 "welcome save" immediately post-onboarding. Send a day-1 anticipation notification.
Tradeoff
A welcome save is slightly artificial — the money comes from somewhere and has to be handled carefully in the accounting. But the organic-wait approach risks losing users before they've seen any proof the product does what it claims.
Decision: A ₹10–₹20 seeded welcome save. Activation was redefined as "first successful save seen by the user," not "app install." That definitional change mattered for cohort analysis — install-to-activation conversion became the leading indicator we tracked in week 1.
D–03
Trust vs. fraud prevention — the withdrawal tension
Problem
Zero-friction withdrawal (the right trust decision) creates a real fraud surface. If a user's account is compromised, a bad actor can drain savings quickly. Biometric re-auth closes that gap but adds friction that signals distrust of the user.
Options
Require biometric re-auth for all withdrawals. Require re-auth above a threshold (e.g., ₹5,000). Rely entirely on backend anomaly detection. Require re-auth only for new destination accounts.
Tradeoff
Every authentication step tells the user their own money is not immediately accessible. For a product where trust is the primary value proposition, that signal is corrosive. But no authentication is also a real risk we can't ignore.
Decision: No re-auth for amounts under ₹10,000 to existing linked accounts. Anomaly detection (withdrawal frequency, device fingerprinting, unusual amounts) runs on the backend. Re-auth is required only when withdrawing to a new or unverified account. This was a deliberate risk acceptance, not an oversight — and it requires tight monitoring in the first 90 days post-launch.
D–04
Simplicity vs. flexibility — no streaks, no fixed goals
Problem
An early design showed a fixed ₹5,000 default goal as a progress bar. It looked like progress visualization. Behaviorally, it created the exact guilt spiral the product promised to avoid — users falling behind an implied pace felt like they were failing at Spare, too.
Options
Fixed goals. User-set goals. No goals. Dynamic milestones (next round number above current balance).
Tradeoff
Goals create motivation peaks but also failure states. Streaks reward consistency but penalize the exact irregular behavior pattern of the target user. A no-streak product can't produce engagement spikes — but also can't produce the shame crashes that drive churn.
Decision: Dynamic milestones only — the next round number above the current balance (₹500 → ₹1,000 → ₹2,500 → ₹5,000). The goalposts always move forward. Nobody is ever behind. Streak mechanics excluded permanently. The research made this obvious: users who felt behind on a goal stopped engaging. Users who felt any progress, however small, kept going.
D–05
What to measure — engagement vs. outcomes
Problem
Standard fintech metrics reward engagement: DAU, session depth, time in app. For Spare, high time-in-app is a bad signal — it likely means the product is confusing, not compelling. But pure outcome metrics miss whether users actually feel differently about money.
Options
Standard engagement metrics. Pure financial outcomes (balance, saves/week). Hybrid: behavioral outcomes plus a qualitative D30 sentiment check.
Tradeoff
Excluding engagement metrics is uncomfortable in investor conversations. Boards want DAU. But optimizing for DAU here would actively hurt the product — it would incentivize adding features that bring users back to the app, which is the opposite of what makes Spare work.
Decision: Explicitly exclude DAU/MAU and session depth. Primary metrics: D30 retention, saves per user per week, average balance at D60. Qualitative anchor: a single D30 in-app prompt — "Do you feel better or worse about saving than a month ago?" Binary response only. The product's job is to shift that answer, and we can't improve what we don't measure.
09 Core User Flow

From install to habit — what happens and why

The flow is built around one principle: every moment that could generate anxiety is handled by the product, not left for the user.

Onboarding · <60 seconds, 2 screens
Permission sequencing matters
Notification permission is requested before bank account access — deliberately. A "yes" on notifications lowers the psychological barrier for the bank ask. No tutorial, no feature walkthrough. The first save notification is the tutorial. Anything more was cut.
Day 1–2 · Cold start
Proving the product works before the user spends anything
The seeded welcome save eliminates the cold-start silence problem. The home screen shows balance, recent saves, and the withdraw button — nothing else. Every element that didn't serve one of those three purposes was removed, not deferred.
Day 7–60 · Habit formation
Milestone moments without gamification
At ₹500, ₹1,000, ₹2,500: warmer notification copy marks progress without badges or confetti. Milestone copy shifts from confirming a transaction to reinforcing identity — "You've become someone who saves." That shift is deliberate and is the product's long-term retention mechanism.
Any time · Withdrawal
Permanently accessible, intentionally visible
The withdraw button is on the home screen, always visible, never in settings. Even users who never tap it benefit from seeing it — the affordance communicates ownership. Users who do tap it must find the experience frictionless. If they don't, the product is over for them. Covered in detail next.
Pause & return
One nudge, no pressure
If saving is paused, one re-engagement notification at day 7. One. No escalation. No urgency framing. If not resumed by day 30, the user is flagged as at-risk in our internal tracking — but they're never told that. The door stays open without pressure.
10 Trust & Withdrawal Experience

The moment most fintech products get catastrophically wrong

Research was unambiguous on one point: the fear that kept users from trusting automatic saving products wasn't about losing money to bad investments or app failures — it was about access. "How do I know I can get it back?" appeared in the data in dozens of variations. This is the anxiety Spare's withdrawal flow exists to resolve before the user ever thinks to ask.

Most fintech products treat withdrawal as a retention threat and respond by adding friction: confirmation modals, "are you sure?" dialogs, biometric re-auth, loss framing ("you'll lose your savings streak"), exit surveys, cooling-off periods. This is a mistake disguised as caution. Each friction element sends the same signal: we don't trust you with your own money.

In India, where the target demographic has lived experience with chit funds and early digital wallets that made money hard to access, that signal triggers the exact distrust that kills a product through word-of-mouth — not a bad review, a conversation. The original PRD treated withdrawal as two sentences. A product review correctly flagged it as the single most dangerous gap in the document.

Users who complete a frictionless withdrawal are more likely to re-engage within 7 days, not less. An easy exit builds more trust than any feature.

How the flow works. Three taps from home screen to confirmed withdrawal. Destination account and transfer timeline are shown on the amount selection screen — before the user confirms, not buried in a tooltip after. One optional reflection line appears below the CTA on full withdrawals: "You've saved ₹{total} since you started — it goes where you need it." It doesn't block anything.

Processing transparency. The balance updates optimistically on confirmation tap, before backend confirmation. Button copy changes to "Sending ₹{amount}..." — the amount stays in view, the user knows what's in motion. At 6 seconds without a response: "Still working on it — almost there." At 5 minutes post-initiation: a push notification confirming the withdrawal is processing. Proactive, not reactive.

The done screen does two jobs. It confirms the transfer. It confirms the product continues: "Spare keeps saving with your next transaction." The second sentence matters as much as the first — without it, users don't know if withdrawing has broken their setup.

Dark patterns this product explicitly avoids:

Pattern Why it's a problem What we do instead
"Are you sure?" confirmation modal Implies the user is making a mistake. Creates doubt about a decision they've already made. No gate. Show a transaction summary and a clear CTA.
Loss framing ("you'll lose momentum") Manufactures guilt. Treats money as belonging to the product, not the user. One warm reflection line, below the CTA, not blocking it.
Exit survey before confirming Delays access to user's own money. Creates resentment. No survey in the flow. Gather this data from cohort analysis post-hoc.
Biometric re-auth for small amounts Security theater for amounts that pose minimal risk. The friction signal outweighs the protection. Re-auth only for new/unverified destination accounts. Anomaly detection on the backend.
Hiding the withdraw option in settings Communicates that the product doesn't want users to find the exit. Destroys trust before a withdrawal even happens. Withdraw is always visible on the home screen, every session.
Principle 01
Visible access = safety
Even users who never tap the withdraw button benefit from seeing it. The affordance itself builds trust passively.
Principle 02
Transparency before commitment
Destination account and timeline shown before confirmation. Not in a tooltip. On the screen, before the user taps.
Principle 03
Proactive communication
A push at 5 minutes post-initiation. A delay notice before 3 business days. The product reaches out before the user has to wonder.
Principle 04
Errors own themselves
"The transfer didn't go through — your ₹{amount} is back in Spare." Not "your transfer failed." The product takes responsibility. Always.
Principle 05
Withdrawal is a use, not an exit
The done screen confirms the product continues. Zero balance is never framed as empty — it's "your savings are with you."
Principle 06
Fraud handled behind the scenes
Anomaly detection runs server-side. Users don't feel the security — it doesn't touch the experience.
11 Microcopy & UX Thinking

Copy as behavioral infrastructure

For a product where users spend almost no time in the app, every word they encounter carries outsized behavioral weight. The research confirmed this clearly: users cited the language of existing apps as a churn trigger, not the features. "It made me feel judged." "It kept reminding me I wasn't doing enough." Copy in Spare is not a communications function — it is the product's primary UX surface.

Save notification · Routine
"₹{amount} quietly set aside. Running total: ₹{total}."
"Quietly" reinforces effortlessness — the core product promise — in a single word. "Running total" keeps the frame on accumulation, not the triggering transaction. The merchant name is absent deliberately: including it would reframe this as a spending alert, not a saving reward. That reframe would undo the emotional architecture the product is built on.
Withdrawal flow · Confirm CTA
"Send to my account"
First-person possessive ("my account") asserts ownership. "Send" is a specific transitive action. The secondary button reads "Change amount" — not "Go back" (spatial regression) or "Cancel" (abandonment framing). Every word in a trust-critical flow is load-bearing.
Withdrawal success · Continuation line
"₹{amount} is on its way. Spare keeps saving with your next transaction."
Two sentences, two jobs. The first confirms without celebration — confetti on a screen that often represents financial necessity would be tone-deaf. The second resets the behavioral frame: the habit loop is intact. Without this line, users don't know if withdrawing has broken their setup. It's the reason users come back after withdrawing.

The notification library maintains 30+ variants stratified by context — routine saves, milestones, morning vs. evening timing — to prevent habituation. Milestone copy deliberately shifts register: from confirming a transaction to reinforcing identity ("You've become someone who saves"). That shift is the product's long-term retention mechanism.

12 Edge Cases & System Thinking

Reliability as a product feature

How a product handles failure determines how much users trust it in normal operation. Each of these was spec'd as a first-class UX moment.

Failed transfers: Balance reverts immediately and visibly. "The transfer didn't go through — your ₹{amount} is back in Spare." One retry offered. Never a generic error. Never a message that implicates the user. Always one visible next step.

Network dropout mid-flow: If the confirmation tap was received before disconnect, proceed optimistically and notify when connectivity returns. If not, return the user to the amount screen with input preserved. Idempotency on the backend prevents double-transfers. Users must never be made to start over.

Delayed transfers: If not confirmed within 3 business days, a proactive push notification with a new ETA and support CTA — before the user has had to ask. Weekend and bank holiday delays are surfaced on the confirm screen, before commitment, not after as a surprise.

The notification cap problem (found in review): The initial spec said "3 notifications per day" without specifying batch behavior. A user with 8+ UPI transactions in a day would have 5 saves receive no feedback — breaking the core reinforcement loop. Resolution: cap at 3 individual notifications per day, then batch remaining saves into a single end-of-day summary: "You saved ₹74 across 6 transactions today."

The reliability principle: Every error state has exactly one clearly visible next step. Every dead end has a way out. The user's money must always be visibly accounted for. These aren't edge case specs — they are trust deposits made in advance.
13 Learnings

What was wrong, what was weak, what we'd do differently

01
The withdrawal flow was an afterthought in V1 — and that was dangerous
The original PRD said withdrawal "should be straightforward" and moved on. In Indian fintech, where users carry acute sensitivity to money being "trapped," underspecifying the exit flow isn't a UX oversight — it's a trust failure in waiting. The research data made this pattern explicit before we built, but the spec didn't reflect it. Learning: trust-critical flows need as much rigor as onboarding. Deferring them to engineering judgment is not acceptable.
02
"Invisible by default" was the wrong product principle
The PRD originally stated "the best Spare experience is one users barely think about." That's wrong as a design principle — it conflicts with how habits form. Habits require a cue-routine-reward loop to internalize. If users never notice Spare working, they never build a saving identity. Acorns proved that silent accumulation without identity formation doesn't produce durable retention. The corrected principle: invisible effort, visible progress. These are different design targets.
03
The save algorithm got one sentence when it needed a section
5–10% capped at ₹50 is the most consequential number in the product. It received one sentence in V1. A structured critique found the flat cap created wildly inconsistent experiences: ₹50 on a ₹12,000 utility bill feels arbitrary; ₹50 on a ₹500 dinner feels meaningful. Fixed obligations weren't excluded, meaning users saw saves on rent — which feels punitive. The algorithm needed a behavioral rationale, a category exclusion list, and a ramp-up mechanic for new users.
04
SMS parsing reliability was underestimated — and should have been a launch blocker discussion
~85–90% accuracy across Indian bank SMS formats sounds acceptable until you realize 1 in 8 transactions generating no save — with no explanation to the user — looks like the product breaking. We launched on SMS parsing because bank API integration added 6+ weeks. That was the right call under the constraints. But monitoring for missed transactions and user communication around gaps should have been spec'd before launch, not scrambled together after week-one feedback.
05
Reddit data is directionally useful, but demographically skewed
The research pipeline was valuable for validating emotional patterns and surfacing language. But Reddit personal finance communities over-index toward financially engaged, English-fluent users — not the avoidant, anxious saver Spare is actually targeting. The insights held up in qualitative sessions, but the research should have been paired earlier with contextual interviews rather than treated as sufficient on its own.
06
Strategic drift will be a continuous fight, not a solved problem
Feature pressure from advisors, investors, and early users started within weeks of the MVP definition. "Can you add a savings goal?" "What about a weekly report?" Each request was reasonable in isolation. Each would have added a user decision or a negative feedback state. The product principles section of the PRD only works if someone is actively using it as a decision filter — not a values statement that lives in a document.
14 Success Metrics

Targets and hypotheses — not guarantees

These are hypotheses we'd validate in the first 60 days, not projections. The numbers are grounded in comparable benchmarks where possible.

60%
D30 retention target
Indian savings apps typically see 20–25% D30 retention. If Spare hits 60%, the passive mechanic is working. If we're at 35%, something in the habit loop is broken — likely notification quality or the cold-start experience.
5+
Auto-saves per user per week
Minimum threshold to confirm the behavioral loop is working. Below 5 indicates transaction detection is failing or the user segment (salaried, regular spending) is miscalibrated.
₹2k
Avg balance at D60
A ₹2,000 balance at 60 days means savings are real enough to matter — and meaningful enough to create switching cost. This is a hypothesis; it depends heavily on the transaction frequency of early adopters.
>50%
Withdrawal-to-return rate (7 days)
The trust metric. If fewer than half of users who withdraw re-engage within 7 days, the withdrawal experience is destroying trust rather than building it — regardless of how good everything else looks.
Binary
D30 sentiment check
"Do you feel better about saving than a month ago?" A user who saved ₹2,000 but still feels anxious has not been served by the product. We track this shift because it's what the product is actually trying to do.
What we explicitly do not measure: DAU/MAU, session depth, time in app. Optimizing for these would incentivize adding features that bring users back to the app — the opposite of what makes Spare work. If time-in-app is rising, that's a flag, not a win.
15 What I'd Do Next

Priorities if D60 retention validates the core loop

Immediate · V1.1
Bank API integration to fix the SMS parsing gap
Addressing the ~10–15% of transactions the SMS parser misses. This improves both save frequency and reinforcement loop reliability. It also enables more accurate transaction categorization, which is the prerequisite for the adaptive algorithm.
V1.1 · Test
Withdrawal-and-return cohort analysis
Track 7-day re-engagement for every user who completes a withdrawal. This is the single best proxy for whether the withdrawal experience builds or destroys trust. If re-engagement is below 50%, the friction analysis starts from the beginning of the flow, not from the apparent problem point.
V1.1 · A/B
Adaptive save rate based on behavioral history
The flat 5–10% algorithm is a starting hypothesis. With 30 days of data, it should adapt to account balance trends and transaction patterns. Primary risk: making the algorithm feel opaque or arbitrary. Mitigation: surface the logic in the notification copy ("We saved a bit less today since your balance was lower").
V2 · Expand
Variable-income user mechanics
The MVP is calibrated for salaried users. For gig workers and freelancers, a 2-week no-save gap during a slow income period looks like product failure to the user — not normal variance. This needs its own behavioral model and its own communication strategy, not a modified version of the salaried flow.
V2 · Identity
Saving identity construction in milestone copy
Behavioral research distinguishes habit (automatic behavior) from identity (self-concept). Spare builds the former but not consistently the latter. Annual savings summaries and milestone moments that reflect who the user is becoming — not just what they've accumulated — are the retention mechanism that's hardest to copy.
